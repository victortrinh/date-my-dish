Choose **Anova** if you want controls directly on the device (no app required), something that behaves like a normal kitchen tool, or a machine backed by a large, well-documented community.

## What sous vide actually is

Sous vide means cooking food in a sealed bag in water held at a precise, consistent temperature. The circulator clips to any pot or container and holds the water within a fraction of a degree of your target.

What makes it different from every other method: the water never exceeds your target temperature, so the food physically cannot overcook. Cooking is edge-to-edge even, with no grey band or dry exterior. And timing is flexible: a steak set at 130 degrees Fahrenheit is still perfect at two hours or at four. The window stays open as long as you need it.

You still finish proteins with a fast, high-heat sear. Sous vide handles the doneness. Searing handles the flavour. Our [sous vide date night guide](/en/articles/sous-vide-date-night/) has the full breakdown if you want temperatures, timings, and a complete date-night game plan.

## The 6 things that actually matter when buying

This is the part that separates "I used it twice" from "this is now my weeknight superpower."

### Controls: on-device vs app

This is less about preference and more about what can go wrong at 8 PM when you are hungry. Anova-style on-device controls work even when your Wi-Fi is being dramatic. You set temperature and time directly on the unit. The failure mode is simple: power off, it stops.

Joule is app-first. The app is polished, but you are opting into a phone workflow. If your phone is dead or the app is glitching, you are annoyed before you have even cooked. Practical test: if you hate connecting your blender to Bluetooth, go Anova.

### Power: heat-up time and recovery

Watts translate to two things: how fast you get to temperature, and how well the circulator recovers when you add cold food or use a bigger container. A small pot for one or two steaks is easy mode. A larger container, especially with bigger batches, punishes weak circulators. If you think you will ever cook for more than two people, do not buy the lowest-power option.

### Temperature accuracy and stability

A good circulator does not just hit 131 degrees. It holds it. Steak is forgiving: a small drift is fine. Salmon is less forgiving. A few degrees in the wrong direction turns silky into dry and sad. Eggs are extremely sensitive: a couple of degrees changes the texture entirely.

When reading reviews, check whether it holds within a tight band over multi-hour tests. "It seemed accurate" is not enough information.

### Pump flow and circulation

Two machines can be equally accurate at the sensor and still cook differently if circulation is weak. In bigger containers, poor flow creates temperature gradients. If you are running more than two bags at once, you want a machine that keeps water moving consistently throughout the bath.

### Clamp design

This is the most underrated decision point. Look for a clamp that fits both thin pot walls and thicker plastic containers, does not slip when condensation builds up, and keeps the device positioned above the minimum water line. A clamp that frustrates you means a machine that sits on the shelf.

### Water level and evaporation

Sous vide is slow cooking. Water disappears. Every circulator has a minimum water line: drop below it and it will alarm or shut off. Evaporation speeds up with hotter temperatures and longer cook times.

The fix is simple: for cooks over two hours, cover the bath loosely. For cooks over six hours, cover it properly with a lid or a dedicated container top. The easiest upgrade is a polycarbonate container with a fitted lid, which keeps temperatures stable and lets you ignore the water level.

The circulator is the brain. Here is what to pair it with on day one.

### A container

Start with a large pot you already own. When you want a dedicated setup, a polycarbonate container with a tight-fitting lid reduces evaporation on long cooks and holds temperature more consistently.

You do not need a vacuum sealer to start. Freezer-grade zip bags with the water displacement method handle everything under four hours: lower the open bag slowly into the water, let the pressure push air up and out, then seal. Zero extra equipment.

## The 4 beginner mistakes that ruin first cooks

Not drying the protein before searing is the most common first mistake. Pat it completely dry with paper towels before it hits the pan. Any moisture left on the surface will steam instead of sear, and the crust you want will not form.

Over-seasoning is the second trap. Flavours concentrate inside a sealed bag. The garlic clove you would use in an open pan becomes aggressive in a bag. Start lighter than feels right: salt, pepper, and one aromatic. You can always adjust at the table.

Floating bags ruin texture. A floating bag means uneven cooking. Use water displacement properly, clip the bag to the side of the container, or add a heavy spoon outside the seal as a weight. If the bag is touching air, it is not cooking.

Choosing the wrong first cook is the last one. Your first sous vide win should be forgiving. Chicken breast at 140 to 145 degrees Fahrenheit, salmon at 120 to 125 degrees, or steak at 129 to 134 degrees for medium-rare. All three are hard to ruin and all three make a strong impression at dinner.
