## What sous vide actually does

Sous vide means cooking food in a sealed bag in water held at a very specific temperature.

The water never goes above your target temp. The food cooks evenly edge to edge. And timing is flexible: you can hold food at temp while you hang out, have a drink, or do something entirely unrelated to cooking. You still finish most proteins with a fast, high-heat sear. Sous vide is the doneness. Searing is the flavor. Together they make something that feels like a restaurant trick but works in your kitchen every single time.

## The 6 things that actually matter when buying

If you want the nerdy breakdown, here's what separates "I used it twice" from "this is now my weeknight superpower."

### 1. Controls and failure modes (app vs on-device)

This is less about preference and more about what can go wrong at 8:07 PM when you're hungry.

Anova-style on-device controls work even if your Wi-Fi is being dramatic. You can set temperature and time directly on the unit, and the failure mode is simple: power off means it stops. Joule's app is genuinely polished, but you're opting into a phone workflow. If your phone is dead or the app is glitching, you're annoyed before you've even cooked.

Practical test: if you hate connecting blenders to Bluetooth, go Anova.

### 2. Power (watts) and heat-up time

Watts translate to two things: how fast you get to temp, and how well the machine recovers when you add cold food or use a bigger water bath. A small pot for one or two steaks is easy mode. A bigger container punishes weak circulators. Beginner rule: if you think you'll ever do bigger batches, don't buy the lowest-power option.

### 3. Temperature accuracy and stability (the whole point)

A good circulator doesn't just hit 131°F. It holds it. Steak is forgiving, and a small drift won't ruin it. Fish is not forgiving: a few degrees can turn silky into flaky and sad. Eggs are extremely sensitive, and a couple of degrees changes the texture completely. Look for reviews that mention holding temp within a tight band over multi-hour tests, not just "it seems accurate."

### 4. Pump flow and circulation

Two machines can be equally accurate at the sensor and still cook differently if circulation is weak. In bigger containers, weak circulation creates temperature gradients and bag placement starts to matter a lot more. If you're ever doing more than two bags at once, you want a machine that keeps water moving well throughout the whole bath.

### 5. Clamp design (more important than you think)

Look for a clamp that fits both thin pot walls and thicker plastic containers, doesn't slip when condensation builds up, and gives the device enough height to stay above its minimum waterline. If the clamp is annoying, you'll avoid using it. That's the real science.

### 6. Water level minimum and evaporation management

Sous vide is slow cooking. Water disappears. Every circulator has a minimum water line: dip below it and it will alarm, shut off, or worse. Evaporation accelerates with hotter cooks and longer times. First-time buyer cheat code: cover the bath for cooks longer than two hours. Cover it properly, with a lid or wrap, for cooks longer than six hours. A dedicated container with a fitted lid makes this effortless.

## The 4 beginner mistakes that ruin first cooks

**1. Not drying before searing.** Dry the surface aggressively with paper towels right before the protein hits the pan. Wet meat steams instead of sears, and you'll lose all the color and crust you're after.

**2. Over-seasoning.** Flavors concentrate in a sealed bag over a long, slow cook. Season a little lighter than you think. You can always finish with more salt and a squeeze of lemon after the sear.

**3. Floating bags.** Floating means uneven cooking. Use the water displacement method properly, clip the bag to the side of the container, or add a spoon as a weight on the outside of the seal. The bag needs to stay submerged.

**4. Starting with the wrong first cook.** Your first win should be forgiving. Good starting points: chicken breast at 140 to 145°F for juicy results every time, [salmon](/en/recipes/quinoa-crusted-salmon/) at 120 to 125°F for that silky texture that's almost impossible any other way, or steak at 129 to 134°F for a perfect medium-rare. Start there. Get confident. Then go further.
