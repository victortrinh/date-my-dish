## What sous vide actually is

Sous vide means cooking food in a sealed bag in water held at a very specific temperature. The water never goes above your target temp. The food cooks evenly edge to edge. And timing is flexible: you can hold food at temperature for hours while you enjoy your evening.

You still finish most proteins with a fast, high-heat sear. Sous vide is the doneness. Searing is the flavor. For a full technique breakdown and a complete date-night game plan with timelines, see the [sous vide date night guide](/en/articles/sous-vide-date-night/).

## The 6 things that actually matter when buying

The difference between "I used it twice" and "this is now my weeknight superpower" usually comes down to one of these.

### 1. Controls and failure modes: app vs on-device

This is less about preference and more about what can go wrong at 8 PM when you are hungry and the chicken has been in the bath for three hours.

The Anova approach (on-device controls) works even if your Wi-Fi is being dramatic. You set temp and time directly on the unit. Power off means it stops. That is the full failure mode.

The Joule approach (app-first) gives you a polished, genuinely well-designed experience, but you are opting into a phone-dependent workflow. If your phone is dead or the app is glitching, you are annoyed before the food even hits the water. Practical test: if you hate connecting your blender to Bluetooth, go Anova.

### 2. Power in watts: heat-up time and recovery

Watts translate to two things: how fast you get to temperature (faster means more likely you will actually use it on a weekday), and how well the circulator recovers when you add cold food or use a bigger container. A small pot for one or two steaks is easy mode. A larger cooler-style setup will punish a weak circulator. If you think you will ever cook bigger batches, do not buy the lowest-power option.

### 3. Temperature accuracy and stability

A good circulator does not just hit 131°F. It holds it. Steak is forgiving: a small drift is not a disaster. Fish is not: a few degrees can turn silky into flaky and sad. Eggs are extremely sensitive: a couple of degrees changes the texture completely. When reading reviews, look for mentions of holding temp within a tight band over multi-hour tests, not just "it seems accurate."

### 4. Pump flow and circulation

Two machines can be equally accurate at the sensor and still cook differently if water movement is weak. In bigger containers, poor circulation creates temperature gradients. Bag placement matters more when the water is not moving well. If you are cooking more than two bags at once, you want a machine that keeps water circulating consistently.

### 5. Clamp design: does it actually fit your life?

This is the most underrated decision point. Look for a clamp that fits both thin pot walls and thicker plastic containers, does not slip when condensation builds, and gives the device enough reach to hit the water while respecting its minimum waterline. If the clamp is annoying, you will avoid using it. That is the real science.

### 6. Water level minimum and evaporation management

Sous vide is quiet cooking, but it is also slow cooking. Water disappears. Every circulator has a minimum water line: dip below it and it will alarm, shut off, or worse, overheat. Evaporation accelerates with hotter cooks and longer times. The rule: if your cook is longer than two hours, cover the bath. Longer than six hours, cover it properly with a lid or wrap. A dedicated container with a lid eliminates this concern entirely.

## The 4 beginner mistakes that ruin first cooks

### Not drying before searing

Dry the surface aggressively with paper towels before it hits the cast iron. Wet meat steams. Dry meat browns. Ten seconds with paper towels is the whole secret.

### Over-seasoning

Flavors concentrate inside a sealed bag. A garlic clove that would disappear into a pan sauce becomes aggressive in a bag. Season a little lighter than you think. Half is usually right.

### Floating bags

A floating bag cooks unevenly because part of the protein never touches the water properly. Fix it with good water displacement when sealing, clip the bag to the side of the container, or slip a spoon outside the seal as a weight.

### Starting with the wrong first cook

Your first win should be forgiving. Chicken breast at 140 to 145°F is juicy and almost impossible to mess up. Salmon at 120 to 125°F is silky and impressive. Steak at 129 to 134°F for medium-rare is the classic. All three handle small timing mistakes well, which matters when you are still figuring out the setup.

Sous vide buys you time. You can start the steak hours before your date arrives, then sear in 90 seconds when you are ready. No scrambling. No overcooked meat. Just a very calm person who clearly has this under control.
