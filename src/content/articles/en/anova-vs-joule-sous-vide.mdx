## Quick start: Anova or Joule?

If you already cook a lot and just want the short answer: get the Anova if you hate apps, get the Joule if you want something compact that stores in a drawer. Done.

I personally use a Joule. I like the size and the app is genuinely good. That said, this isn't a loyalty piece. It's a "which one fits your actual life" piece, and those two things are not always the same.

A quick note on the rest of the market: cheaper sticks can technically work, but they tend to be louder, clunkier, and more annoying to live with day to day. The Anova and Joule aren't perfect, but they're the two you're least likely to regret.

Go with the Anova if on-device controls matter to you (no app required, ever), or if you want something that feels like a regular kitchen tool with a large community of users you can steal recipes and troubleshooting tips from.

Go with the Joule if you want the most compact option that actually fits in a kitchen drawer, don't mind a phone-first workflow, and care about having a polished app with guided cook times. ## What sous vide actually is

Sous vide means cooking food in a sealed bag in water held at a very specific temperature. The water never goes above your target temp. That's the whole trick.

Because the temperature is fixed, the food cooks evenly all the way through and you can hold it there for a while without it overcooking. You're not hovering. You're not guessing. You're doing something else while the machine does the work.

You still sear at the end. Sous vide handles the doneness. A screaming hot pan handles the crust. If you want to go deeper on the technique before buying anything, the [sous vide date night guide](/en/articles/sous-vide-date-night/) covers the full approach.

## The six things that actually matter when buying

If you want the nerdy breakdown, this is what separates "I used it twice" from "this is my weeknight superpower."

**1. Controls and failure modes.** This is less about preference and more about what can go wrong at 8:07 PM when you are hungry. Anova-style devices work even if your Wi-Fi is being dramatic. You set the temp and time directly on the unit, and the failure mode is simple: power off means it stops. The Joule is app-first. The app is genuinely polished, but you are opting into a phone workflow. If your phone is dead or the app is glitching, you are annoyed before you have even cooked. Practical test: if you hate connecting appliances to Bluetooth, go Anova.

**2. Power and heat-up time.** Watts translate to two things: how fast you reach your target temperature, and how well the machine recovers when you add cold food or use a bigger container. A small pot for one or two steaks is easy mode. A larger setup, especially a cooler-style container, punishes weak circulators. If you think you will ever do bigger batches, do not buy the lowest-power option.

**3. Temperature accuracy and stability.** A good circulator does not just hit 131°F. It holds it. This matters more for some proteins than others. Steak is forgiving; a small drift will not ruin it. Fish is less forgiving; a few degrees can turn silky into flaky and sad. Eggs are extremely sensitive; a couple of degrees changes the texture completely. Look for reviews that mention holding temp within a tight band over multi-hour tests, not just "it seems accurate."

**4. Pump flow and circulation.** Two machines can be equally accurate at the sensor and still cook differently if circulation is weak. In bigger containers, poor flow creates temperature gradients. Bag placement matters more when water is not moving well. If you are doing more than two bags at once, you want a machine that keeps water circulating properly.

**5. Clamp design and fit.** This is the most underrated decision point. Look for a clamp that fits both thin pot walls and thicker plastic containers, one that does not slip as condensation builds up, and enough height for the device to stay above the minimum waterline. If the clamp is annoying to use, you will avoid using the machine altogether. That is the real science.

**6. Water level and evaporation.** Sous vide is quiet cooking, but it is also slow cooking. Water disappears. Every circulator has a minimum water line; dropping below it will trigger an alarm, a shutdown, or worse. Evaporation accelerates with hotter cooks and longer times. Simple rule: if the cook is longer than two hours, cover the bath. Longer than six hours, cover it properly with a lid or a dedicated container top.

## Four beginner mistakes that ruin first cooks

**Not drying before searing.** Pat the protein dry aggressively with paper towels before it hits the pan. Wet surface steams instead of sears. You didn't just hold that chicken at 145°F for an hour to finish it with a gray crust.

**Over-seasoning.** The bag traps flavors and concentrates them. Season a little lighter than you think you need to.

**Floating bags.** A bag that floats isn't cooking evenly. Use the water displacement method properly, clip the bag to the side of the container, or drop a spoon in as a weight (outside the seal). It sounds fussy but takes ten seconds.

**Picking the wrong first cook.** Start with something forgiving: chicken breast at 140 to 145°F, salmon at 120 to 125°F, or steak at 129 to 134°F for medium-rare. All three are hard to mess up once you're holding temperature.
