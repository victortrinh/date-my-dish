## What Sous Vide Actually Is

Sous vide means cooking food in a sealed bag in water held at a very precise temperature. The water never goes above your target temp. The food cooks evenly edge-to-edge. And because timing is flexible, you can hold food at temp while you hang out, finish getting ready, or let a bottle of wine breathe.

You still finish most proteins with a fast, high-heat sear. Sous vide handles the doneness. Searing handles the flavor. Two-step cooking that looks like you spent all day on it.

## The 6 Things That Actually Matter When Buying

This is what separates "I used it twice" from "this is now my weeknight superpower."

**1. Controls and failure modes.** This is less about preference and more about what can go wrong at 8:07 PM when you're hungry. Anova works even if your Wi-Fi is being dramatic; you set temp and time directly on the unit. Joule's app is genuinely polished, but you're opting into a phone workflow. If your phone is dead or the app is glitching, you're annoyed before you've even cooked. If you hate connecting blenders to Bluetooth, go Anova.

**2. Wattage and heat-up time.** Watts translate to two things: how fast you get to temp, and how well the circulator recovers when you add cold food or use a bigger water bath. A small pot for one or two steaks is easy mode. A bigger container punishes weak circulators. If you think you'll ever do larger batches, don't buy the lowest-power option.

**3. Temperature accuracy and stability.** A good circulator doesn't just hit 131F. It holds it. Steak is forgiving; a small drift won't ruin it. Fish is not forgiving; a few degrees can turn "silky" into "flaky and sad." Eggs are extremely sensitive. Look for reviews that mention holding temp within a tight band over multi-hour tests, not just "it seems accurate."

**4. Pump flow and circulation.** Two machines can be equally accurate at the sensor and still cook differently if circulation is weak. In bigger containers, weak circulation creates temperature gradients. Bag placement matters more when circulation is poor. If you're doing more than two bags at once, you want a machine that keeps water moving well.

**5. Clamp design and geometry.** This is the most underrated decision point. Look for a clamp that fits both thin pot walls and thicker plastic containers, one that doesn't slip when condensation builds. If the clamp is annoying, you'll avoid using the machine. That's the real science.

**6. Water level and evaporation management.** Sous vide is quiet, slow cooking, and water disappears. Every circulator has a minimum water line; dip below it and it will alarm or shut off. Evaporation accelerates with hotter cooks and longer times. First-timer rule: for anything longer than two hours, cover the bath. For anything longer than six hours, cover it for real with a lid, wrap, or a proper container lid.

## The 4 Beginner Mistakes That Ruin First Cooks

**1. Not drying before searing.** Pat the protein aggressively dry with paper towels right before it hits the pan. Wet meat steams instead of sears, and steaming after a long, careful cook is a tragedy.

**2. Over-seasoning.** Flavors concentrate in a sealed bag during cooking. Season a little lighter than you normally would going in, then taste and adjust after the sear.

**3. Floating bags.** A floating bag means the protein isn't fully submerged, which means uneven cooking. Fix it: use the water displacement method properly, clip the bag to the side of the container, or add a spoon outside the seal as a weight.

**4. Starting with the wrong first cook.** Your first win should be forgiving. Good starting points: chicken breast (juicy at 140 to 145F), salmon (silky at 120 to 125F), steak (129 to 134F for medium-rare). These proteins showcase what sous vide does best before you graduate to eggs or fish.
