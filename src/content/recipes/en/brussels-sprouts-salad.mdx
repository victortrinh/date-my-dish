Most salads know their place. This one doesn't. Fried Brussels sprout leaves that shatter on contact, dark shaved kale dressed in a fish sauce vinaigrette that hits every flavor note at once, torn herbs that smell like a street market, and paper-thin purple daikon for a little extra provocation. It's an appetizer that makes people stop mid-conversation, fork in hand, and ask what they're eating. Tell them it's a salad. Watch their face.

The standard Brussels sprouts salad is raw shaved sprouts, lemon dressing, done. It's fine. It tastes like lunch in a building with a lot of glass. This recipe does something smarter: it treats the same vegetable as two different ingredients.

The outer leaves peel off and go into hot oil, where they turn into something closer to chips than salad. Impossibly light. Audibly crispy. The shaved hearts get a quick fry too, softer, a little tender in the middle, which gives you two textures from one vegetable before you've even touched the dressing. That's the move. Everything else flows from there.

## The fish sauce vinaigrette, and why it matters

Palm sugar melts into fresh lime juice for the base: sweet-tart, a little tropical. Garlic adds heat. Then fish sauce walks in and the whole thing changes.

Fish sauce gets nervous looks from people who haven't cooked with it much. It smells pungent in the bottle and completely transforms once it hits acid and sugar. What you get in this dressing is not fishy: it's deep, savory, and genuinely hard to stop eating. The kind of flavor that makes you reach for another forkful even after you've already decided you're done. If there's a person coming to dinner you want to impress, this is the dressing that does it quietly and without announcing itself.

## Herbs are not optional here

Thai basil, mint, and cilantro are structurally important. Pull them and you have fried vegetables in good dressing. Keep them and you have something worth remembering.

Mint cools everything down, playing against the richness of the vinaigrette. Thai basil brings a soft anise note that pairs with lime the way two people who shouldn't work together somehow do. Cilantro ties the whole plate together with bright, citrusy freshness. Tear the herbs by hand rather than chopping: you release the oils without bruising the leaves. The aroma is noticeably better, and the plate smells alive in a way that matters before the first bite. Be generous. This salad rewards it.

## The fry: less intimidating than it sounds

No batter. No breading. You drop dry leaves into 350°F oil and wait about ninety seconds. That's it.

The leaves bubble immediately, go golden, and come out grease-free in a way that feels almost suspicious. At 350°F, surface moisture evaporates almost instantly, as food scientist Harold McGee notes in *On Food and Cooking*, and that rapid steam escape is what prevents oil from soaking in, leaving you with chips instead of grease. Ninety seconds of hot oil and they're essentially chips. The secret is small batches: overcrowding drops the oil temperature, and the window between crispy and limp is shorter than you'd think. Drain on paper towels, salt immediately while the oil is still on the surface, and don't look away.

The shaved hearts need an extra minute since they're denser. You're looking for light golden edges with a little give in the center. That contrast between shatter-crisp leaves and the slightly tender hearts is what makes this more interesting than just a bowl of fried things.

Crunch has a shelf life, so the order of operations here is worth getting right. Dress the kale and shaved sprout hearts first. Sturdy greens handle the vinaigrette without collapsing, and a few minutes of contact softens the kale into something silky. Mound it generously on your plate.

Quick-toss the purple daikon in the residual dressing at the bottom of the bowl, then layer it over the greens. That color against the dark kale is genuinely striking, and the peppery bite adds one more thing to notice.

The fried leaves go on last. Always last. They lose their crunch the second they touch dressed greens, so place them right before the plate hits the table. Your date takes a bite, the leaves shatter, and that sound is doing more work than you think.

## What to serve it with

This works as a standalone [appetizer](/en/recipes/category/appetizer/) before something lighter: grilled fish, coconut curry, a simple roast chicken. If you want a plant-forward main to follow, our [cauliflower steak with romesco sauce](/en/recipes/cauliflower-steak-with-romesco-sauce/) has the same kind of vegetable-first confidence. Or start the table with [crispy vegan calamari](/en/recipes/crispy-vegan-calamari/) alongside and let the contrast between fried crunch and dressed greens do the talking. The acid in the dressing cleanses the palate well, and the herbs keep the aftertaste fresh rather than heavy.

Want to make it a full meal? Double the recipe and serve over steamed jasmine rice. The dressing pools at the bottom and works as a light sauce over rice in a way that makes you wish you'd made more.

If you want a dessert that plays off all that savory brightness, [lemon posset brulee](/en/recipes/lemon-posset-brulee/) is a natural close to the evening.

## Ingredient notes worth keeping

Palm sugar has a caramel complexity that brown sugar can approximate but doesn't fully replicate. It comes in discs or jars at most Asian grocery stores and dissolves into lime juice cleanly. Worth the detour if you're near one. If not, light brown sugar is a real backup, not a compromise.

The sunflower-olive oil blend keeps the dressing from going too assertive. Pure olive oil has opinions that can crowd out the herbs. Straight sunflower or grapeseed oil does the job if you don't have a blend.

For the kale: lacinato is the best choice, flat leaves that absorb evenly and don't fight back. Curly kale brings more volume and crunch, though its ruffles tend to grab too much dressing. Either way, pull the tough stems and tear the leaves rather than cutting them.

This is the salad that gets people to reconsider the whole category. One bite, that shatter, the lime-fish sauce punch, the herbs. No one's calling it rabbit food after that.
