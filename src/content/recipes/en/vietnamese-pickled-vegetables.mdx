Growing up with a Vietnamese mom in Montreal meant our fridge always had a jar of do chua in it. Every Sunday she'd make a huge batch, and by Wednesday it was half gone. Mostly my doing. I'd pry open the jar and steal spoonfuls straight from the brine when nobody was watching, and I have zero regrets about this.

When I moved out and started cooking for dates, do chua was the first thing I figured out how to make myself. Not because it's complicated (it isn't), but because I quickly noticed what happened to a meal when I put a little bowl of it on the table. People who'd never had it before would take one bite, stop talking, and reach for more without thinking. That pause is worth everything. It's the detail that makes you look like you really know what you're doing in the kitchen, even when the main dish is just grilled chicken and rice.

Fifteen minutes of prep. A night in the fridge. A jar that makes every meal feel considered.

## What do chua actually is

Do chua translates roughly to "sour stuff" in Vietnamese, which is both modest and completely accurate. It's julienned daikon and carrots in a sweet-vinegar brine. That's it. The condiment is foundational to Vietnamese cooking: you find it on banh mi, next to grilled meats, tucked into spring rolls, spooned over rice bowls. It shows up where other cuisines would use coleslaw or pickled onions, except it's crunchier, brighter, and more fun.

The reason it works so well with rich food is simple: the acid cuts through fat and resets your palate between bites. Rich pork belly, fatty pate, a heavy braise. Do chua is what stops any of those from becoming too much. A condiment that earns its place on the table by making everything else taste better is rare. This one does it without asking for credit.

Six ingredients. The ratio between them matters more than any of them individually.

Daikon is non-negotiable. It's the large ivory-white root you'll find at any Asian grocery store, or at Jean-Talon Market if you're in Montreal. The flesh is mild and faintly peppery raw, and it mellows into something almost sweet once it's spent a day in brine. More importantly, it stays crunchy. Red radishes are not a substitute: they're too small, too sharp, and they go soft in a way that ruins the whole point.

Carrots bring sweetness and that orange-and-white color contrast that makes a finished jar look almost too good to open. Almost. They also pickle at a slightly different rate than daikon, so you get a little textural variation in every bite.

White vinegar, not rice vinegar and not apple cider. Rice vinegar is too gentle for this; apple cider pulls the flavor in the wrong direction. Regular white vinegar gives you the clean, direct acidity that makes do chua taste like do chua.

## Cut it right

Consistent matchsticks, about 5 cm long and 3 mm thick. That's what you're going for. Uniform pieces pickle at the same rate and you get even crunch throughout, rather than some bits that are perfect and some that went too far.

A julienne peeler is the fastest tool here and produces beautiful, regular strips with almost no effort. A mandoline works too. If you're using a knife, sharpen it first and take your time. Irregular cuts aren't a disaster but you'll notice the difference, and once you've had properly cut do chua you won't want to go back.

Toss the cut vegetables with salt and leave them for 20 minutes. You'll see water start pooling at the bottom of the bowl almost immediately. That's exactly what you want. Osmosis pulls moisture out of the vegetables so the brine doesn't have to fight with it later.

If you skip this step, the vegetables hold onto that water and release it into the brine once they're submerged. Your carefully measured vinegar-to-sugar ratio gets diluted. The result is soft, watery pickles that taste flat and lack any snap. Not worth it.

After the 20 minutes: rinse thoroughly under cold running water for a full minute, then dry completely. A salad spinner is fastest. A clean kitchen towel works too. Get them as dry as you can, because any lingering water still dilutes the brine. Dry vegetables make clean pickles. Wet vegetables make excuses.

## The brine ratio

The brine is 1 part vinegar to 2 parts water, sweetened enough to soften the vinegar without tasting sugary. Stir until every sugar crystal is dissolved; undissolved sugar sinks and creates inconsistent pickling. If you use warm water to dissolve the sugar faster, let it cool before adding the vegetables. Hot brine wilts things, and wilted do chua is a waste of a good daikon.

The sugar isn't decorative. It balances the vinegar, rounds out the flavor, and plays a small role in preservation. Cut it by more than 25% and the brine turns one-note: aggressive, sour-forward, and the kind of thing that bullies everything else on the plate. A little sweeter than the recipe is fine if that's your taste. Noticeably less sweet is not.

The classic is banh mi: a baguette piled with Vietnamese cold cuts or pork, pate, jalapenos, cilantro, and a generous handful of do chua. If you've only ever eaten banh mi without homemade pickles, you're going to feel a little cheated once you try it this way.

Beyond the sandwich, do chua belongs almost anywhere you'd use a sharp, crunchy condiment. Spoon it over rice bowls with grilled chicken or caramelized pork. Pile it alongside our [quinoa-crusted salmon](/en/recipes/quinoa-crusted-salmon/) for a contrast that genuinely works. Use it in place of coleslaw in Korean-style tacos. Put it on the table next to a Friday-night fried egg and rice and call it a proper meal.

It's also good on a cheese board, if that sounds surprising. A little acidity and crunch next to something fatty and rich is exactly what a good charcuterie spread needs. Do chua does that job well and looks interesting doing it.

Do chua keeps for up to 3 weeks in the fridge and genuinely improves over the first few days. By day three the vegetables have soaked up the brine fully and the flavor has rounded out from sharp to something more complex and balanced. Day one is good. Day three is better. Day five is when you start making a second jar before the first one runs out.

Use glass. Plastic absorbs vinegar smell and gives it back to the pickles over time. A 1-litre mason jar is the right size for this batch.

The recipe scales without issues. Double it, triple it, same ratios. Some weeks I keep two jars going at staggered stages so there's always a fully developed one ready to open. If you want more ideas for what to make alongside it, the full collection of [side-dish recipes](/en/recipes/category/side-dish/) has plenty to work with.

The first time you serve homemade do chua to someone who hasn't had it before, watch what happens after the first bite. There's a little pause. Then they reach back in. That's the whole reason to make a fresh jar every week.
