Growing up with a Vietnamese mom in Montreal meant our fridge always had a jar of these pickled vegetables. Every Sunday she would make a massive batch, and by Wednesday it was half gone — mostly because I'd sneak spoonfuls straight from the jar. When I eventually moved out and started cooking for dates, I quickly realized that do chua was the secret ingredient separating a good meal from a great one. Sandwiches, rice bowls, grilled meats — they all needed that bright, tangy crunch to feel complete.

## The Magic of Do Chua

Do chua translates loosely to "sour stuff" in Vietnamese, which is both charmingly understated and exactly accurate. This simple condiment of julienned daikon radish and carrots pickled in a sweet vinegar brine is foundational to Vietnamese cooking. It shows up on banh mi, alongside grilled meats, tucked into fresh spring rolls, and spooned over rice bowls. Its genius lies in contrast — the crunchy vegetables and sharp brine cut through rich, fatty foods with surgical precision, refreshing the palate with every bite.

Making do chua from scratch tells your date something. It says you know what you're doing in the kitchen, that you think ahead, and that you care about the details. A jar in the fridge is like mise en place for your whole week of cooking.

The ingredient list is refreshingly short: daikon, carrots, salt, white vinegar, water, and sugar. That's it. What makes this recipe work is the quality of each component and the ratio between them.

**Daikon radish** is the backbone of do chua. It's that large, ivory-white root vegetable you'll find at any Asian grocery store or at the Jean-Talon Market here in Montreal. Daikon has a mild, slightly peppery flavour that mellows beautifully in the brine, and its firm flesh holds its crunch far better than softer vegetables. Do not substitute regular red radishes — they're too small, too sharp, and will turn unpleasantly soft.

**Carrots** bring natural sweetness and a vivid pop of orange that makes the finished jar look as good as it tastes. They also pickle at a slightly different rate than daikon, so the combination gives you subtle variation in texture within each bite.

**White vinegar** is non-negotiable here. Apple cider vinegar is too fruity, rice vinegar too mild. Plain white vinegar gives you the clean, sharp acidity that defines authentic do chua without competing with the vegetables' natural flavours.

## The Julienning Step: Precision Pays Off

Consistent size is the quiet hero of this recipe. When your matchsticks are uniform — roughly 5 cm long and 3 mm thick — they pickle at the same rate and deliver an even texture in every forkful. A julienne peeler is the fastest tool for the job and produces beautifully thin, regular strips in minutes. A mandoline with a julienne blade works equally well. If you only have a knife, sharpen it and take your time; irregular pieces will pickle unevenly.

Toss the julienned vegetables with salt and let them rest for 20 minutes. Within a few minutes you'll see water pooling at the bottom of the bowl — that's osmosis at work, drawing the natural moisture out of the vegetables. This pre-treatment is not optional.

Here's why it matters: if you skip this step and go straight to pickling, the vegetables release their water content into the brine, diluting the carefully balanced vinegar-to-sugar ratio and producing pickles that are waterlogged and soft. The salting step guarantees that your vegetables enter the brine already dehydrated, ready to absorb the pickling liquid cleanly rather than releasing their own. After the 20 minutes are up, rinse thoroughly under cold running water — at least a full minute — to remove all the salt, then dry completely in a salad spinner or with a kitchen towel.

## Balancing the Pickling Brine

The brine is a 1:2 ratio of vinegar to water with enough sugar to round off the sharpness. Stir until the sugar is fully dissolved; undissolved sugar settles to the bottom and creates an uneven pickle. If you use slightly warm water to speed dissolution, let the brine cool to room temperature before adding the vegetables — hot brine wilts them.

The sugar level is calibrated to balance the vinegar without making the pickles taste sweet on their own. If you reduce the sugar too aggressively (more than 25%), the brine becomes one-dimensionally sour and loses the rounded character that makes do chua so addictive.

Do chua is one of those rare condiments that improves almost everything it touches. The most iconic use is on banh mi, piled high alongside pork, pâté, jalapeños, and fresh cilantro. But its applications extend well beyond the sandwich.

Spoon it over rice bowls alongside grilled chicken or pork for instant brightness. Use it in place of coleslaw in Korean-style tacos. Add a small mound alongside our [quinoa-crusted salmon](/en/recipes/quinoa-crusted-salmon/) for a Southeast Asian-inspired contrast. It's equally at home with a simple fried egg over jasmine rice on a weeknight as it is on a date night cheese board alongside other pickled and fermented things. Think of it as the Vietnamese version of giardiniera — it belongs everywhere.

Do chua keeps in the refrigerator for up to 3 weeks in a sealed glass container — and it gets better as the days pass. The flavours develop and mellow, and by day three the vegetables have fully taken on the brine's character. This makes it one of the best make-ahead condiments in any cook's arsenal.

Always use glass over plastic. Plastic containers absorb the vinegar smell and can leach odours back into the pickles over time. A 1-litre mason jar is ideal for this batch size.

This recipe scales effortlessly. Double or triple the quantities using the same ratios, and you'll have enough to share with a neighbour or keep two jars going at different stages of development.
