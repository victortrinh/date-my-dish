Growing up with a Vietnamese mom in Montreal meant our fridge always had a jar of these pickled vegetables. Every Sunday she would make a massive batch, and by Wednesday it was half gone — mostly because I would sneak spoonfuls straight from the jar when nobody was looking. When I eventually moved out and started cooking for dates, I realized something: do chua was the quiet secret separating a good meal from one that made people lean back and say "okay, what else can you cook?" Sandwiches, rice bowls, grilled meats — they all needed that bright, tangy crunch to feel complete. This is the condiment that punches above its weight, every single time.

## The Magic of Do Chua

Do chua translates loosely to "sour stuff" in Vietnamese, which is both charmingly understated and exactly accurate. Julienned daikon and carrots in a sweet vinegar brine — that is it. And yet this simple condiment is foundational to Vietnamese cooking. It shows up on banh mi, alongside grilled meats, tucked into spring rolls, and spooned over rice bowls with the kind of quiet confidence that says "I belong here."

Its genius is contrast. The crunchy vegetables and sharp brine cut through rich, fatty foods with surgical precision, refreshing your palate with every single bite. Making do chua from scratch tells the person across the table something important: you think ahead, you care about the details, and you know that the best meals are built from the small things done well. A jar in the fridge is not a condiment — it is mise en place for your entire week.

Six ingredients. No fancy equipment. No excuses not to make it this weekend. What makes do chua work is the quality of each component and the ratio between them — get those right and the recipe practically makes itself.

**Daikon radish** is the backbone. That large, ivory-white root vegetable you will find at any Asian grocery store or at the Jean-Talon Market here in Montreal. Daikon has a mild, slightly peppery flavor that mellows beautifully in the brine, and its firm flesh holds its crunch far longer than softer vegetables. Regular red radishes? Absolutely not — too small, too sharp, and they go soft in a way that will disappoint you.

**Carrots** bring natural sweetness and a vivid orange that makes the finished jar look almost too pretty to eat. Almost. They also pickle at a slightly different rate than daikon, so each bite has subtle textural variation that keeps things interesting.

**White vinegar** is non-negotiable. Apple cider vinegar is too fruity, rice vinegar too mild. Plain white vinegar delivers the clean, sharp acidity that defines authentic do chua. It does not compete — it just does its job perfectly.

## The Julienning Step: Precision Pays Off

Here is where your knife skills earn their keep. Consistent matchsticks — roughly 5 cm long and 3 mm thick — mean everything pickles at the same rate and you get an even texture in every forkful. A julienne peeler is the fastest tool and produces beautifully thin, regular strips in minutes. A mandoline works equally well. If you only have a knife, sharpen it first and take your time. Irregular pieces pickle unevenly, and uneven pickles are a conversation nobody wants to have.

Toss the julienned vegetables with salt and walk away for 20 minutes. Within moments you will see water pooling at the bottom of the bowl — that is osmosis doing exactly what you need it to do, pulling moisture out of the vegetables so the brine does not have to compete with it later.

This step is non-negotiable, and here is why: skip it, and the vegetables dump their water content straight into the brine, diluting the carefully balanced vinegar-to-sugar ratio. The result? Waterlogged, limp pickles that taste like disappointment. The salting step ensures your vegetables arrive at the brine already dehydrated, primed to absorb the pickling liquid cleanly. After the 20 minutes, rinse thoroughly under cold running water for at least a full minute to wash away the salt, then dry completely in a salad spinner or on a kitchen towel. Wet vegetables dilute the brine. Dry vegetables make perfect pickles. It really is that simple.

## Balancing the Pickling Brine

The brine is a 1:2 ratio of vinegar to water, with enough sugar to soften the vinegar's edges without making things taste sweet. Stir until every crystal dissolves — undissolved sugar settles at the bottom and creates an uneven pickle, which is a problem nobody should have to troubleshoot. If you use warm water to speed dissolution, let it cool to room temperature before adding the vegetables. Hot brine wilts them, and wilted do chua is a contradiction in terms.

The sugar level is calibrated, not arbitrary. Cut it by more than 25% and the brine becomes one-dimensionally sour — sharp, aggressive, and no fun to eat on a banh mi. The sugar is what gives do chua its addictive, rounded character. Respect the balance.

Do chua is the rare condiment with zero ego and unlimited range. Its most iconic role is on banh mi — piled high alongside pork, pate, jalapenos, and fresh cilantro. But its applications stretch far beyond the sandwich.

Spoon it over rice bowls with grilled chicken or pork for instant brightness. Swap it in for coleslaw in Korean-style tacos. Pile a small mound alongside our [quinoa-crusted salmon](/en/recipes/quinoa-crusted-salmon/) for a Southeast Asian contrast that makes the whole plate sing. It is equally at home with a fried egg over jasmine rice on a Wednesday night as it is on a date-night cheese board next to other pickled and fermented things. Think of it as the Vietnamese giardiniera — it belongs everywhere, and it makes everything better.

Do chua keeps in the fridge for up to 3 weeks — and it improves every day it sits there. By day three the vegetables have fully absorbed the brine's personality, and the flavors have mellowed into something genuinely addictive. This is the ultimate make-ahead condiment: future you will be very grateful to present you.

Always use glass. Plastic absorbs the vinegar smell and eventually leaches it back into the pickles, which is exactly as unpleasant as it sounds. A 1-litre mason jar is perfect for this batch size.

The recipe scales effortlessly — double or triple the quantities using the same ratios. You will have enough to share with a neighbor, or better yet, keep two jars going at different stages of development so you never run out.
