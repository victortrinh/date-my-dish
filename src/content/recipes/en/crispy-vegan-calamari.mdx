The question usually comes mid-chew. "Wait. Is this actually calamari?" And then a pause, a squint, another bite. That's the moment this recipe was built for. Golden rings, batter that shatters on contact, a meaty interior with just enough chew: king oyster mushrooms doing their best impression of squid, and absolutely nailing it. Thirty minutes, no seafood, one little secret ingredient that most people have never heard of.

Not every mushroom is up for this. Button mushrooms turn to mush the second they hit hot oil. Shiitakes are too thin and too fragrant. King oyster mushrooms have thick, cylindrical stems, dense enough to slice clean, firm enough to hold their shape through a soak, a batter, and a full fry. The texture is what makes it work: that familiar slight resistance when you bite through, the one that makes your brain say "seafood" before your taste buds have even weighed in.

The flavor is mild on purpose. Real calamari isn't about intense fish taste; it's about texture and batter, with a faint hint of the sea underneath. King oysters provide the texture. The batter handles everything else. And the kombu water soak adds that last piece: a quiet oceanic note that makes the whole thing click. Your date doesn't need a full explanation. Let them eat first.

## The kombu soak, which is doing more work than it looks like

Kombu is dried seaweed, the same stuff that forms the backbone of dashi in Japanese cooking. In 1908, Japanese chemist Kikunae Ikeda at Tokyo Imperial University isolated glutamic acid from kombu broth and identified it as the source of a distinct savory taste he named "umami," publishing his findings in the *Journal of the Chemical Society of Tokyo*. That single discovery changed how we understand flavor. Soak a four-inch strip in a cup and a half of room-temperature water for 30 minutes, and the liquid picks up that same glutamic acid. The water turns faintly yellow. It smells lightly of the ocean. That's exactly what you want.

The mushroom rings soak in this liquid for 15 to 20 minutes before they meet the batter. Long enough to absorb some of that briny depth. This is the step that separates "interesting fried mushrooms" from "what is happening, why does this taste like calamari." You can find kombu at any Asian grocery store, often in the dried goods section. It keeps forever. Buy a pack and you'll use it.

Skip the kombu and the recipe still works as a snack. Keep it and the recipe works as a trick.

## Getting the batter right

Flour gives the batter structure. Cornstarch is what makes it shatter. That clean, crispy crack when you bite through is the cornstarch doing its job: it fries up lighter and crisper than flour alone, and it holds that crunch longer as things cool down. Baking powder adds a little lift, keeping the coating airy rather than dense.

The seasoning is Mediterranean and deliberate. Dried oregano is the dominant note: earthy, slightly floral, the smell of Italian calamari fritti. Black pepper brings warmth. Cayenne adds heat that builds slowly, just enough to make the next bite interesting. Together they smell like a restaurant kitchen in a good way.

Add water to the dry ingredients gradually and stop when it looks like pancake batter: thick enough to coat a spoon, thin enough to drip slowly. Too thick and you end up with a doughy jacket. Too thin and it slides off in the oil. The right consistency coats the rings evenly and fries up golden in about four to five minutes.

## Frying without wrecking it

Oil temperature is everything here. At 350°F, the batter hits the oil and sets almost immediately, trapping moisture inside and building that crust on the outside. Below that, the batter absorbs oil instead of crisping up. Above it, the outside burns before the inside is done. Use a thermometer. It takes 30 seconds and it matters.

Lower the rings carefully, one at a time, and use a wooden spoon to nudge apart any that stick together in the first few seconds. Then step back. Two minutes on the first side, undisturbed. One flip. Another two to three minutes. That's your window.

Fry in two batches. I know it's tempting to do it all at once. Don't. Overcrowding drops the oil temperature and you get pale, greasy rings instead of golden ones. The second batch is just as good as the first if you keep the oil hot. Both batches land on paper towels, then straight to the plate.

## Serving them while they're still worth eating

These are a right-out-of-the-fryer situation. The crunch fades as they sit, so get them on the table fast. A squeeze of lemon goes on immediately while they're still glistening, because the acid cuts through the oil and makes everything taste brighter. Flaky sea salt on top. Parsley if you have it. Done.

For dipping, marinara is the obvious move and it's obvious for good reason: oregano in the batter, tomato in the sauce, they understand each other. Garlic aioli works if you want something richer. Sriracha mayo if heat is the point. A small bowl of each and you've built an [appetizer](/en/recipes/category/appetizer/) that looks like you put in considerably more effort than 30 minutes. ## Fitting them into a bigger dinner

The calamari works on its own as a starter, but it also plays well before something substantial. Pair it with our [Brussels sprouts salad](/en/recipes/brussels-sprouts-salad/) for a crispy-meets-fresh combination that covers texture from every angle. Follow with [cauliflower steak with romesco sauce](/en/recipes/cauliflower-steak-with-romesco-sauce/) for a fully plant-based main, and close with [lemon posset brulee](/en/recipes/lemon-posset-brulee/) for dessert. Three courses, entirely plant-based, and nobody at the table will bring it up because there's nothing to notice.

For groups, double the batch and fry in waves, bringing fresh rings to the table as they finish. The sizzle from the kitchen keeps the anticipation going. These are the kind of appetizers that make people forget to check their phones.

## The part where they figure it out

Most people guess what they're eating somewhere between the first and third ring. By then they've already committed to enjoying it, which is the whole game. Good food doesn't need a reveal or a story. It needs to taste good enough that the reveal happens on its own. King oyster mushrooms, a handful of pantry ingredients, and one bag of dried seaweed. That's all this is. The confidence comes from knowing it works.
