These chips never make it to the table. Paper-thin zucchini and eggplant, coated in a sparkling water batter so light it barely registers, fried until they shatter at first bite. You plate them with the best of intentions. Then you turn around to grab the tzatziki and half the stack is gone. This Mediterranean appetizer takes 15 minutes of active cooking, no culinary training, and one cold bottle of club soda. The only thing it asks of you is a hot pan and a little patience with the oil temperature.

## Why sparkling water changes everything

Most people treat the liquid in batter as an afterthought. It isn't. Swap still water for cold club soda and you get a completely different result. The dissolved CO2 creates thousands of tiny bubbles locked inside the flour mixture. The second those bubbles hit hot oil, they expand hard and fast, pushing the batter outward into a shell that's airy and crunchy instead of dense and doughy.

There's a second effect worth knowing: carbonation slows gluten development. Less gluten means a more delicate, tender crust that holds its crunch rather than turning tough as it cools. This is the same logic behind good tempura batter, which uses ice-cold water for similar reasons. Sparkling water does both jobs at once.

The catch: it only works cold. Room-temperature sparkling water has already lost most of its fizz. Keep the bottle in the fridge until the exact moment you're ready to mix. This is not a step you can prep ahead of dinner and walk away from.

Half a medium zucchini and half a small Italian eggplant. That's it. Italian eggplant gets the nod over globe eggplant because it has fewer seeds and a denser flesh that holds up to frying without going soft in the middle. The chip comes out cleaner.

Slice both paper-thin, ideally 1/16 to 1/8 inch. A mandoline earns its counter space here: uniform slices cook at exactly the same rate, so every chip exits the oil evenly golden. If you're slicing by hand, sharpen the knife first and go slowly. Uneven slices mean some chips burn before others are done, which is the kind of thing that makes you question your choices at the pan.

The warm-water soak sounds unnecessary but isn't. It draws a small amount of moisture to the cut surface, which helps the flour coating grip instead of sliding off. Shake off the excess well: you want the slices damp, not wet. The flour coat creates a dry barrier between vegetable and batter, and that barrier is what keeps the batter crispy rather than steaming from the inside out.

The dry mix takes about thirty seconds. All-purpose flour handles the structure. Cornstarch adds crunch and slows the crust from softening after frying. Baking powder gives extra lift that works alongside the carbonation. Dried oregano pulls the whole thing into Mediterranean territory, and paired with zucchini and eggplant, it's genuinely good. The kitchen smells like a seaside taverna, which is never a bad thing.

Rice flour is listed as optional. It isn't, really. One tablespoon gives the crust a noticeably crunchier, more brittle texture because rice flour absorbs less oil than wheat. If it's in your pantry, it goes in the bowl.

Pour in the cold sparkling water gradually and whisk until the batter coats the back of a spoon. Thicker than crepe batter, thinner than pancake batter. Lumps are fine. More than fine: over-mixing develops gluten, which is exactly what you're trying to avoid. Stop early. The chips will tell you you made the right call.

## Frying for maximum crunch

Heat oil to 350F in a heavy-bottomed saucepan. Everything in frying hinges on consistent temperature. Clip a thermometer to the side and don't guess. The oil drops a few degrees when you add food, so starting a touch above your target helps absorb that dip without pulling you out of range.

Dip each floured slice in batter, let the excess drip off for a beat, and lower it into the oil. Three or four slices at a time. Crowding drops the temperature and the chips steam instead of crisping: nobody wants that. Give each one room to float and sizzle.

Two to three minutes per batch, usually. Watch the edges go golden and the bubbling slow down. Slower bubbling means surface moisture has cooked off, which is the sign the chip is nearly done. Pull with a slotted spoon, drain, and season with sea salt while the surface is still hot and oily. Salt sticks to hot oil. On a cold chip, it just falls off.

## Serving with tzatziki

Tzatziki is the right call here: cool, garlicky, a little tangy. It's the perfect counterpoint to hot, crispy, oregano-forward chips. The temperature contrast is most of the reason this appetizer works so well. No tzatziki on hand? A squeeze of lemon and some flaky salt will cover you.

Serve these the moment they come out of the oil. All fried food has a window of peak texture, and these chips have a shorter one than most. Bring them straight to the table, stack them casually, put the tzatziki alongside. The whole presentation should feel like something you'd order at a tiny spot near the water, the kind where the menu only has six things and everything's good.
