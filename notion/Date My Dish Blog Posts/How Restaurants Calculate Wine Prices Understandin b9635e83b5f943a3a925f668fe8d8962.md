## The Basic Markup Structure

Restaurants typically use one of two pricing models:

### Model 1: Flat Multiplier (Most Common)

**Wholesale cost × 2.5 to 4 = Restaurant price**

Example:

- Restaurant buys wine for $20 wholesale
- Marks it up 3×
- Sells for $60

**Why this matters:** You're paying $40 markup on a $20 bottle.

---

### Model 2: Tiered Markup

**Lower-priced wines:** Higher multiplier (3-4×)

**Mid-priced wines:** Moderate multiplier (2.5-3×)

**High-priced wines:** Lower multiplier (2-2.5×)

Example:

- $15 wholesale → $60 retail (4× markup)
- $50 wholesale → $140 retail (2.8× markup)
- $200 wholesale → $450 retail (2.25× markup)

**Why this matters:** Expensive wines offer relatively better value per dollar spent.

## What You're Really Paying For

The markup isn't just profit. It covers:

**Storage costs:** Climate-controlled wine storage

**Inventory risk:** Wine that doesn't sell or spoils

**Glassware:** Quality glasses, washing, replacement

**Service:** Sommelier knowledge, opening, pouring

**Ambiance:** The experience of dining out

**Typical breakdown:**

- 30% cost of wine
- 25% labor
- 20% overhead (rent, utilities)
- 15% other costs (glassware, breakage)
- 10% profit

**Translation:** Restaurants make modest profit on wine despite high markups.

## The Retail Comparison

**Quick math:** Find retail price, multiply by 2-2.5 for expected restaurant price.

Example:

- Bottle retails for $25 at wine shop
- Fair restaurant price: $50-60
- If restaurant charges $90: Overpriced

**Why this works:** Retail already includes markup from wholesale. Restaurant markup should be on wholesale price, not retail.

## Where Value Hides

### Strategy 1: Second-Cheapest Wine

**Avoid:** Cheapest wine on list

**Why:** Restaurants know people avoid looking cheap. The cheapest wine often has the highest markup.

**Choose:** Second or third cheapest in each category

**Why:** Better quality, slightly lower markup percentage, still affordable.

---

### Strategy 2: House Wine Can Be Good

**When it works:** Restaurants with serious wine programs

**Red flag:** Generic "house red" with no details

**Green flag:** Specific producer named ("Our house Chianti is from XYZ winery")

**Why:** Good restaurants choose house wines they're proud to serve.

---

### Strategy 3: Lesser-Known Regions

**Overpriced:** Napa Cabernet, Burgundy, Bordeaux, Champagne

**Better value:**

- Chile, Argentina (similar quality, lower wholesale cost)
- Spanish wines (Rioja, Ribera del Duero)
- Portuguese wines
- Southern Italian wines

**Why:** Lower demand means lower wholesale prices and lower markups.

---

### Strategy 4: Older Vintages

**Opportunity:** Wines from several years ago

**Why:** Restaurant bought at lower prices, hasn't adjusted for current market

**Risk:** Check vintage quality before ordering (Google "[year] [region] vintage")

## The By-the-Glass Game

### How It Works

**Standard:** 5 glasses per bottle

**Restaurant pricing:** Each glass = 1/3 to 1/2 bottle price

Example:

- Bottle costs restaurant $20
- Sells for $60 by bottle
- Sells for $14-18 per glass
- 5 glasses = $70-90 total

**The math:** Restaurants make more per bottle selling by the glass.

---

### When to Order by the Glass

✅ **You're having one drink**

✅ **You want to try multiple wines**

✅ **You don't want a full bottle**

### When to Order a Bottle

✅ **You'll drink 3+ glasses between you**

✅ **You want better quality (BTG options are limited)**

✅ **You want to choose from full list**

**Break-even:** If you'll drink 3-4 glasses, bottle is better value.

## Red Flags (Overpriced Wine Lists)

❌ **No wines under $60-70**

Suggests aggressive pricing across the board

❌ **Common wines at 4-5× retail**

Example: Wine you know retails for $20 selling for $100

❌ **All wines from famous regions**

Napa, Burgundy, Bordeaux markups are highest

❌ **Vague descriptions**

"House Red" with no producer = lowest quality at high markup

❌ **Outdated vintages at current prices**

Old vintage should be discounted, not at current market price

---

## Green Flags (Fair Wine Programs)

✅ **Variety of price points**

Something good at $40, $60, $80+

✅ **Lesser-known regions represented**

Shows sommelier knowledge, offers value

✅ **Detailed descriptions**

Producer, region, vintage, tasting notes

✅ **Half bottles available**

Shows commitment to providing options

✅ **Sommelier on staff**

Knowledgeable person who can guide choices

## The BYO Option

Some restaurants allow you to bring your own wine.

**Corkage fee:** $15-50 to open your bottle

**When it makes sense:**

- Special bottle you own
- High-markup restaurant
- Corkage fee under $30

**Etiquette:**

- Call ahead to confirm policy
- Don't bring wine they already sell
- Offer sommelier a taste
- Tip on what wine would have cost

**Math:**

- $40 retail wine + $25 corkage = $65 total
- Same wine on list would be $100-120
- Savings: $35-55

## Date Night Strategy

### Before You Arrive

**Research the wine list online:** Many restaurants post it

**Check retail prices:** Know what wines cost at wine shops

**Set a budget:** Decide what you're comfortable spending

**Look for reviews:** Mentions of wine program quality

---

### At the Restaurant

**Ask for recommendations:** "What's drinking well tonight in the $60-80 range?"

**Mention food:** "We're having steak and fish, what would work for both?"

**Point to price:** If uncomfortable saying amount, point to price on list "Something in this range"

**Don't rush:** Taking time shows you're thoughtful, not indecisive

---

### What to Say

✅ **"What do you recommend in this price range?"**

Direct, shows you have budget in mind

✅ **"What's the best value on your list?"**

Sommeliers respect this question

✅ **"I usually like [style], what's similar?"**

Helps them guide you

❌ **"What's cheap?"**

Comes across as penny-pinching

❌ **"Why is this so expensive?"**

Confrontational, awkward

## The Confidence Move

Ordering the second-cheapest wine while appearing confident about your choice:

**Frame it:** "I've heard good things about [region/producer]" or "This should pair nicely with what we're having."

**Body language:** Decisive, not apologetic

**Tone:** Matter-of-fact, not defensive

**Result:** Looks like informed choice, not budget concern

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Can I negotiate wine prices?**

No. Prices are fixed. But you can ask if they have a similar wine at lower price point.

**Q: Should I tip on wine?**

Yes. Standard tip (18-20%) applies to entire bill including wine.

**Q: Is it rude to ask about prices?**

No. Asking "What's good value?" or "What's your house wine?" is completely acceptable.

**Q: What if the wine is corked (spoiled)?**

Tell server immediately. They should replace it at no charge. This happens, it's not your fault.

**Q: Can I return wine if I don't like it?**

Depends on restaurant. If obviously flawed, yes. If just not to your taste, usually no (unless they're very accommodating).

## The Psychology of Wine Pricing

Restaurants know:

- People avoid cheapest wine (looks cheap)
- People avoid most expensive (looks pretentious)
- Most order in second-cheapest to middle range
- Strategic markups concentrate on this zone

**Your advantage:** Knowing this lets you find actual value, not perceived value.
